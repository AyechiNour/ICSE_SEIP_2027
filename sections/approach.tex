\section{Proposed Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

Given the challenges of locating business rules in large and complex software systems, we propose an automated approach to support developers in this task. The following sections present our approach and describe its main steps.

\subsection{Overview of the Approach}

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Schéma.png}
    \caption{Overview of the proposed approach}
    \label{fig:approach}
\end{figure*}

To address the challenge of locating business rules in large codebases, we propose an automated approach that combines source code analysis and documentation to narrow the search space. The approach relies on linking the \textit{business terms} used in rule descriptions to \textit{business entities} in the source code. Following~\cite{Wang08b,Snee21a,Ayac26a}, we establish these links and use them to identify methods that manipulate the relevant entities. In this work, business entities comprise business classes and their attributes.

Figure~\ref{fig:approach} illustrates our automated approach. It comprises two preliminary steps, performed once for the entire codebase to identify business entities and the methods that manipulate them, and four rule-specific steps, applied to each business rule to map its vocabulary to relevant entities, select candidate methods, and refine the results using documentation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preliminary Steps}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item[\textbf{P.1}] Identify business entities in the source code.
        \item[\textbf{P.2}] Identify the methods that manipulate each business entity.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Rule-Specific Steps}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item[\textbf{R.1}] Extract business vocabulary from the business rule description.
        \item[\textbf{R.2}] Map business vocabulary to the entities identified in P.1.
        \item[\textbf{R.3}] Select the methods identified in P.2 that manipulate the entities mapped in R.2.
        \item[\textbf{R.4}] Refine the candidate methods using information extracted from the documentation.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

The mapping in R.2 can be ambiguous because multiple entities may have identical or similar names while representing the same or related business concepts. Since a business term may correspond to several entities, R.2 retains all relevant matches rather than selecting a single entity. Accordingly, R.3 applies an OR condition: a method is retained if it manipulates at least one of the mapped entities. This strategy favors coverage by reducing the risk of excluding relevant implementations due to ambiguous mappings, although it may increase the number of candidate methods.

To address this potential increase, R.4 further refines the candidate set using information extracted from the documentation. While R.2 and R.3 prioritize retaining relevant methods, R.4 helps narrow the search space by removing methods that are less likely to implement the business rule. The overall objective is to balance coverage and search-space reduction, providing developers with a manageable set of candidate methods for further investigation rather than attempting to identify a single implementation point.

\subsubsection{Detailed Steps}\noindent

The remainder of this section describes each step in detail and explains how it contributes to the identification of methods that potentially implement a given business rule.

\textbf{Preliminary Steps}

\textbf{P.1 — Identify business entities in the source code}

We first identify the business entities present in the source code. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:businessVar}, business variables can provide useful information for locating business concepts in the code. However, identifying such variables in a large industrial system would require examining a number of program elements. In an object-oriented system, classes provide a more structured view of the concepts implemented in the application, while variables provide more detailed information about them. We therefore use business classes as the starting point for identifying business entities.

To identify these classes automatically, we rely on information available from both the source code and the underlying database. We consider classes belonging to the Object-Relational Mapping (ORM) layer that expose or manipulate data persisted in the database as business classes. These classes provide access to persistent data and represent the main business concepts implemented in the application. This criterion allows us to identify the relevant classes without manually examining a large number of program elements.

For each identified business class, we also consider its attributes as business entities. Attributes provide more detailed information about the business concepts represented by their corresponding classes. By considering both the classes and their attributes, we capture business concepts at two levels of granularity: the class level and the attribute level.

At the end of this step, we obtain a set of business entities composed of the business classes and their corresponding attributes.

\textbf{P.2 — Identify the methods that manipulate each business entity}

In this step, we consider two types of business entities: classes and attributes. For each business class, we collect the methods that reference the class, as these methods may implement operations involving the corresponding business entity. For each business attribute, we identify the methods that invoke its accessors, namely its getter and setter methods.

At the end of this step, we obtain a collection that associates each business entity with a list of methods.

\textbf{Rule-Specific Steps}

\textbf{R.1 — Extract business vocabulary from the business rule description}

Given a description of a business rule, we aim to identify the business vocabulary it refers to. In a context where no dictionary is available to define the vocabulary specific to the domain. There is no boundary between domain-specific business terms and general words, and the same term may have different meanings depending on the context in which it appears. Moreover, a business rule may contain types of words, including domain concepts, actions, conditions, and contextual information, which makes it difficult to determine which terms are relevant for locating their implementation. Therefore, the identification process must distinguish between terms that refer to business concepts and terms that do not.

Unlike~\cite{Ayac26a}, which uses Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques to extract business terms from business rule descriptions, our approach does not rely on NLP for this task. This is because business terms do not necessarily belong to a specific linguistic category: they may correspond to individual nouns, noun phrases, or other linguistic structures. Consequently, defining a fixed set of rules to identify all business terms can be challenging.

Automatically identifying business terms from the rule description may lead to two opposite problems: retaining words that are not relevant to the business domain or discarding terms that are actually useful for locating the rule implementation. We therefore adopt a conservative strategy. Rather than selecting only the terms that are initially considered to be business terms, we retain a broad set of possible candidates extracted from the rule description.

For each business rule description, we first remove stop words and then generate chunks corresponding to consecutive sequences of the remaining words. These chunks cover different combinations of words that may form a business term, ranging from individual words to longer expressions. This strategy allows us to preserve potential business terms even when their linguistic structure cannot be determined in advance. As a result, the generated list may contain both relevant chunks that represent business concepts and irrelevant ones that do not correspond to meaningful business terms. The latter are filtered out in the subsequent steps using additional information available from the source code. At this stage, the objective is therefore not to precisely identify business terms, but rather to preserve the information contained in the rule description and avoid prematurely excluding potentially relevant candidates.

For example, consider the following business rule description: \textit{``If specified, the issuing budget must exist for the local authority''}. We first remove the stop words from the sentence. The resulting sequence of words is \textit{``specified issuing budget exist local authority''}. We then generate consecutive chunks from this sequence by progressively extending each starting word with the following words. Starting from \textit{specified}, for example, we obtain \textit{specified}, \textit{specified issuing}, \textit{specified issuing budget},\ldots up to \textit{specified issuing budget exist local authority}. We then start with the second word, \textit{issuing}, and generate \textit{issuing}, \textit{issuing budget},\ldots up to \textit{issuing budget exist local authority}. Similarly, starting from \textit{budget}, we obtain \textit{budget}, \textit{budget exist},\ldots The same process is repeated for \textit{exist}, \textit{local}, and \textit{authority}, resulting in all possible consecutive chunks of the remaining words.

At this stage, all generated chunks are retained as candidates, without determining whether they correspond to actual business terms. For example, \textit{budget} and \textit{local authority} may correspond to business concepts, whereas \textit{specified} and \textit{exist} may not. However, both relevant and potentially irrelevant chunks are retained at this stage. This conservative strategy allows us to preserve potentially relevant business terms without requiring linguistic rules to determine their relevance in advance. The filtering of irrelevant chunks is performed in the subsequent steps using the business entities identified in the source code.

At the end of this step, all generated chunks are candidates.

\textbf{R.2 — Map business vocabulary to the entities identified in P.1}

Each chunk obtained from the previous step, R.1, is converted into a numerical vector, referred to as an embedding, using a text-embedding model. An embedding provides a numerical representation of the semantic content of a textual expression, allowing expressions with similar meanings to be compared even when they do not use exactly the same words. Similarly, each business entity identified in P.1 is represented by an embedding generated from its name. This provides a common numerical representation for the business vocabulary extracted from the rule description and the business entities identified in the source code.

For each chunk, we compute its cosine similarity with the embeddings of all business entities. The similarity scores quantify the semantic similarity between the chunk and each business entity. We then rank the business entities according to their similarity scores, from the most similar to the least similar. Thus, each chunk is associated with a ranked list of business entities that are potentially relevant to the business concept expressed by that chunk.

We then apply two parameters to filter the chunks and their associated business entities. First, we use a similarity threshold to determine whether a chunk is sufficiently related to at least one business entity. The cosine similarity score ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater semantic similarity between a chunk and a business entity. We discard a chunk if its highest similarity score is below the selected threshold, as this indicates that the chunk is not sufficiently related to any business entity. Based on our experiments, we selected a threshold of \textbf{0.7}. Lower thresholds resulted in weaker semantic matches, introducing noise by retaining chunks that were only loosely related to the business entities. Conversely, a threshold of \textbf{0.7} provides a stricter selection criterion while accommodating lexical and syntactic differences between business terms in documentation and their representations in the source code. This threshold therefore offers a balance between filtering out weak matches and retaining relevant associations despite differences in how business terms are expressed in documentation and code.

Second, for each remaining chunk, we determine how many similarity scores should be retained. We experimented with retaining the entities corresponding to the top-1, top-2, and top-3 similarity scores. Based on these experiments, we selected the top-3 similarity scores. Thus, for each chunk, we retain the business entities associated with the three highest similarity scores. This choice allows us to preserve several relevant business entities while limiting the amount of irrelevant information introduced at this stage.

At the end of this step, each retained chunk is associated with the business entities corresponding to its three highest similarity scores, which represent the closest semantic matches.

\textbf{R.3 — Select the methods identified in P.2 that manipulate the entities mapped in R.2}

The goal of this step is to filter the methods in the code and retain only those that are likely to implement a given business rule. To this end, we rely on the results of step P.2, which provides a list of methods associated with each business entity, as well as the list of business entities identified from the rule description in step R.2.

We retain methods associated with at least one business entity from the list obtained from the chunks of the rule description. This process removes methods that manipulate other business entities with no apparent connection to the rule, resulting in a smaller set of candidate methods for further analysis. Consequently, developers do not need to inspect the entire codebase but can focus their investigation on methods that are potentially related to the implementation of the business rule.

The primary objective of this step is to retain as many relevant methods as possible rather than eliminate all irrelevant ones. Since methods discarded at this stage cannot be recovered in later steps, it is essential to ensure that the method implementing the business rule remains among the retained candidates. Some irrelevant methods may therefore be preserved, even if they do not ultimately contribute to the implementation of the rule. This trade-off is intentional: the filtering process should reduce the size of the search space while minimizing the risk of excluding the actual implementation method. Thus, even when the number of candidate methods is reduced, we aim to ensure that the method implementing the business rule remains within the resulting set of candidates.

\textbf{R.4 — Refine the candidate methods using information extracted from the documentation}

The business-rule documentation provides additional contextual information that can be used to further refine these candidates. As described in Section~\ref{sec:industrialContext}, each documentation file has a meaningful title and is organized into sections that provide contextual information about the corresponding business rules. These elements provide clues about the business context of a rule and the classes in which its implementation may be located.

This step uses this information as an additional filtering criterion. Rather than reconsidering the complete source code, it focuses only on the methods identified by the preceding steps and examines the classes in which they are implemented. The objective is to determine whether these classes are consistent with the context provided by the business-rule documentation.

More specifically, we extract the contextual information available in the documentation, namely the file title and the section title associated with the business rule. We then compare these titles with the names of the classes containing the candidate methods. A method is retained when the name of its containing class contains a term corresponding to the identified documentation context. Otherwise, it is removed from the candidate set.

For example, consider a business rule documented in a file entitled \texttt{Budget}. The title indicates that the rule is related to the \texttt{Budget} business concept. If a candidate method is implemented in a class such as \texttt{BudgetService} or \texttt{BudgetMethod}, the class name is consistent with this context, and the method is retained. Conversely, a method implemented in a class whose name does not reflect the identified context is discarded.

This step further filters the candidates produced by the preceding steps. While the preceding steps use the business entities and terms expressed in the rule to identify potentially relevant methods, this step exploits the organizational context of the documentation to further refine these candidates.
