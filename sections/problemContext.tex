\section{Industrial Context and Problem Setting}
\label{sec:industrialContext}

Locating business rules in large-scale software systems is a common industrial challenge, as these rules are often embedded in source code without explicit links to their textual descriptions. This challenge is also encountered at Berger-Levrault, a major software company specializing in information systems for public administration, healthcare, and education. The company maintains large and long-lived software systems in which business rules are intertwined with technical implementation details and distributed across the codebase. Over time, business rules may change, requiring developers to update their implementations in the source code. Identifying the code that needs to be modified can be difficult and time-consuming, especially when there is no explicit link between a business rule description and its implementation. Failing to identify the appropriate implementation may result in incomplete or incorrect changes to the system. In this work, we investigate this issue through an industrial case provided by Berger-Levrault, using one of its legacy software systems to evaluate our approach.

The industrial case considered in this work is a large-scale financial management system developed and maintained by Berger-Levrault. The system is a long-lived object-oriented Java application that supports business processes related to financial management. Over the years, it has evolved through continuous maintenance and the addition of new functionalities, resulting in a large and complex codebase. In this context, business rules are implemented across different parts of the system and are often mixed with technical and application-specific logic. This makes the system a relevant setting for studying the problem of locating the code that implements a given business rule.

The size of the system further illustrates the scale of the problem. The analyzed version of the application contains \textbf{16,555 classes} and \textbf{115,829 methods}. The codebase contains several types of classes that serve different purposes. In this work, we focus on the classes that are directly related to business concepts and their implementation, rather than considering all classes in the system.

Among these classes, \textbf{561} are persistent business entity classes. These classes represent business entities and inherit from a common superclass, \texttt{AbstractPersistentEntity}, which provides a shared structure for persistent entities. For example, the \texttt{Budget} class inherits from \texttt{AbstractPersistentEntity} and represents a business entity related to financial management. It contains a set of attributes describing the entity, together with the corresponding getter and setter methods.

In addition to these persistent entity classes, the system contains service-oriented classes that contain methods involved in the implementation of business rules. These classes can often be identified by their names, which include terms such as \texttt{Service} or \texttt{Method}. In the analyzed version of the system, \textbf{1,080 classes} belong to this category. For example, the \texttt{BudgetService} and \texttt{BudgetMethod} classes contain methods that handle business logic. The methods defined in these service classes are therefore potential locations for business-rule implementations. Overall, the analyzed codebase contains \textbf{14,578 methods}.

An important characteristic of this codebase is the consistency of its business vocabulary. Class names are largely based on terms related to financial management and reflect the concepts used by the application. For example, the term \texttt{Budget} appears in the names of several classes, such as \texttt{Budget}, \texttt{BudgetService}, and \texttt{BudgetMethod}. This shared vocabulary creates a link between a persistent entity and the methods of the corresponding service classes. At first sight, this link may suggest that business rules concerning the \texttt{Budget} entity can be found in \texttt{BudgetService} or \texttt{BudgetMethod}.

However, this assumption does not always hold. A business rule may involve several persistent entities. Therefore, relying only on the names of persistent entity classes does not allow us to precisely identify where the rule may be implemented, as several business entities may be involved. Moreover, even when a business rule is related to a single entity, the corresponding service classes can still contain a large number of methods. For example, \texttt{BudgetService} and \texttt{BudgetMethod} together contain \textbf{559 methods}. The size of these classes also illustrates the difficulty of using the class name alone as a search indicator: \texttt{BudgetService} contains \textbf{18,441 lines of code}, while \texttt{BudgetMethod} contains \textbf{502 lines of code}.

Therefore, although the shared business vocabulary provides a useful connection between persistent entities and service classes, it does not by itself provide sufficient information to efficiently locate the implementation of a business rule. This characteristic helps narrow the search space, but the problem remains challenging, even when considering only the classes that follow this naming consistency. In addition, some classes do not follow this naming pattern, making the correspondence between business entities and service classes less direct. Therefore, a more precise filtering strategy is needed to further reduce the number of candidate methods.

The system is also accompanied by extensive documentation that contains descriptions of the business rules implemented in the source code. However, the documentation does not generally provide explicit links between a business rule and the methods that implement it. In a few rare cases, such links are available. We use these explicitly linked business rules as ground truth for the evaluation of our approach.

The business rules are organized in a set of text files. Each file has a title and contains several sections. Each section groups a set of business rules in a two-column table. The first column contains an identifier for the rule, while the second column provides its textual description. The titles of the files and sections are meaningful and generally reflect the business concepts described in the rules. For example, a file may have \texttt{Budget} as its title and contain a section named \texttt{User Account}. This section then contains a set of business rules, each identified by a rule identifier and followed by its textual description. The terminology used in the document titles and section names is also close to the terminology used in the source code.

This observation provides another possible way to narrow the search space. We assume that a business rule may be implemented in a service class whose name contains either the name of the file or the name of the section in which the rule is documented. For example, a rule described in the \texttt{Budget} file or in the \texttt{User Account} section could potentially be implemented in a service class whose name contains \texttt{Budget} or \texttt{UserAccount}.

However, this information alone does not solve the localization problem. Identifying a particular service class can reduce the search space, but the situation is similar to the one observed when using class names from the source code. The selected service classes may still contain a large number of methods, leaving a substantial number of candidate methods to examine. The main advantage of using the documentation is that, when a business rule involves several business entities, the file and section names can help identify the service classes associated with these entities and provide a more targeted starting point for the search.

Another limitation comes from the difference between the terminology used in the documentation and the names used in the source code. The documentation is written in natural language, whereas class names depend on the naming practices adopted by developers. Developers may use abbreviations, different word forms, or other naming conventions that do not exactly match the terminology used in the documentation. As a result, it may not always be possible to directly identify the corresponding service class from the name of a file or section.

Therefore, the documentation provides a useful first level of filtering, but it is not sufficient to precisely locate the implementation of a business rule in a large and evolving codebase.

This limitation can be explained by the evolution of the system over time. As new functionalities are added and existing business rules evolve, the corresponding classes and methods become increasingly large and numerous. Information that may have been sufficient to locate an implementation when the system was smaller may therefore become less effective as the system grows.

Finally, each business rule in the documentation is associated with a unique identifier. For a small number of rules, this identifier can be explicitly linked to its implementation in the source code. In these cases, the rule identifier appears either in the name of a method or in a comment associated with the method, such as a comment above the method or within its body. These explicit links provide reliable rule--implementation pairs. We use these pairs as the ground truth for evaluating our approach and determining whether it correctly identifies the method implementing a given business rule.

This industrial context highlights the need for automated approaches that can help developers locate business rules directly in the source code. Such approaches can reduce the effort required to identify relevant implementations and support maintenance activities without relying solely on source code or on documentation.
