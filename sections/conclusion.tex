\section{Conclusion And Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Locating the implementation of business rules in large and evolving systems remains a challenging task, particularly when documentation is incomplete and the relationship between business requirements and source code is not explicitly established. In this work, we presented a fully automated approach that assists developers in this task by progressively reducing the search space of potential implementation methods.

The approach establishes a link between business rule descriptions and source code through the identification of business entities associated with the concepts expressed in the rule. These entities are then used to identify methods that manipulate or access them, resulting in a set of candidate methods for further investigation. We further introduced a documentation-based refinement that exploits contextual information available in business-rule documentation to narrow the candidate set.

The evaluation conducted on a large industrial software system shows that the approach can retain the relevant implementation for most of the evaluated business rules while considerably reducing the number of candidate methods. The results also show that contextual information from the documentation can further reduce the candidate set, with only a small decrease in the proportion of correctly retained implementations. These findings demonstrate the potential of combining semantic information from business rule descriptions with information extracted from source code and documentation to support business-rule localization in legacy systems.

The approach is not intended to automatically identify a unique implementation of a business rule. Instead, it aims to provide developers with a focused and manageable set of candidate methods, thereby facilitating the investigation of large codebases. Several directions can be explored to further improve the approach and evaluate its practical usefulness.

First, the current evaluation is based on a single industrial system and a reference dataset of 100 business rules. Evaluating the approach on additional industrial systems from different domains would provide stronger evidence regarding its generalizability and its applicability to systems with different architectures, naming conventions, and documentation practices.

Second, future work could investigate whether the reduction in candidate methods translates into a measurable reduction in developer effort. A controlled developer study could evaluate task completion time, cognitive effort, or the number of code elements inspected when locating the implementation of a business rule. Such an evaluation would provide a more direct assessment of the practical benefits of the approach for software maintenance.

Finally, future work could investigate ranking the retained candidate methods according to their likelihood of implementing the business rule. While the current approach deliberately preserves multiple candidates to avoid discarding relevant implementations, a ranking mechanism could help developers prioritize their investigation. Additional information available from the source code, business rule descriptions, and documentation could also be exploited to improve this ranking. This would extend the approach beyond search-space reduction toward more precise and developer-oriented business-rule localization.
