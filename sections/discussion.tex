\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Evaluation scope}\noindent

Although our approach can be applied to the entire source code, we evaluate it on a subset of 14,578 methods identified as responsible for business logic. This choice provides a more meaningful assessment of the approach by focusing on the methods that developers would reasonably consider when searching for a business rule implementation. The complete codebase also contains various technical components, such as test code, constructors, accessors, persistence-related methods, and infrastructure or utility code, that are not expected to implement business rules. Including these methods in the initial search space would artificially lower the Recovery Effort Index (REI), as many could be excluded without analyzing the rule description. Restricting the evaluation to business-logic methods therefore provides a more informative measure of the approach's ability to narrow down potential implementations.

\paragraph{Diversity of business rules}\noindent

Although the evaluation covers 100 business rules, these rules span \textbf{12 distinct document titles} and \textbf{22 distinct section titles}, covering different parts of the documentation and a variety of business concepts and vocabularies. The descriptions contain \textbf{167 distinct business terms}, further illustrating the diversity of the terminology used across the rules. This diversity provides insight into the behavior of the approach across different types of rules, while the variation in candidate-set sizes reflects differences in how business concepts are expressed in the documentation and represented in source code.

\paragraph{Terminology mismatch between rules and source code}\noindent

The approach successfully retains the reference method in 94 of the 100 evaluated cases. The six unsuccessful cases highlight the difficulty of establishing reliable links between business-rule descriptions and source-code terminology. More specifically, four of these six cases relate to R.2, where differences between the terms used in business-rule descriptions and the names of business entities in the source code may lead to incorrect entity matching. Consequently, the correct business entity may not be retained, preventing the approach from identifying the reference implementation.

\paragraph{Documentation and source-code alignment}\noindent

The effectiveness of the approach also depends on the correspondence between the available documentation and the organization of the source code (R.4). This issue arises in two of the six unsuccessful cases. In these cases, the titles of documentation files or sections do not directly match the names of the classes containing the relevant implementations. As a result, contextual information may be insufficient to identify the appropriate classes, potentially excluding relevant methods from the candidate set.
