\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Evaluation scope}\noindent

Although our approach can be applied to the entire source code, we evaluate it on a subset of 14,578 methods identified as responsible for business logic. This choice provides a more meaningful assessment of the approach by focusing on the methods that developers would reasonably consider when searching for a business rule implementation. The complete codebase also contains various technical components, such as test code, constructors, accessors, persistence-related methods, and infrastructure or utility code, that are not expected to implement business rules. Including these methods in the initial search space would artificially lower the Recovery Effort Index (REI), as many could be excluded without analyzing the rule description. Restricting the evaluation to business-logic methods therefore provides a more informative measure of the approach's ability to narrow down potential implementations.

\paragraph{Diversity of business rules}\noindent

Although the evaluation covers 100 business rules, these rules span different parts of the documentation and involve diverse business concepts and vocabularies. They are not limited to a single functional area or a homogeneous set of business terms. This diversity provides insight into the behavior of the approach across different types of rules, while the variation in candidate-set sizes reflects the differences in how business concepts are expressed in the documentation and represented in source code.

\paragraph{Terminology mismatch between rules and source code}\noindent

The approach successfully retains the reference method in 94 of the 100 evaluated cases. The six unsuccessful cases highlight the difficulty of establishing reliable links between business-rule descriptions and source-code terminology. More specifically, these cases relate to R.2, where differences between the terms used in business-rule descriptions and the names of business entities in the source code may lead to incorrect entity matching. Consequently, the correct business entity may not be retained, preventing the approach from identifying the reference implementation.

\paragraph{Documentation and source-code alignment}\noindent

The effectiveness of the approach also depends on the correspondence between the available documentation and the organization of the source code (R.4). For example, the names of documentation files or sections may not directly match the names of the classes containing the relevant implementations. In such cases, contextual information may be insufficient to identify the appropriate classes, potentially excluding relevant methods from the candidate set. More flexible matching criteria could help accommodate differences in naming and organization while preserving the benefits of using documentation context to identify potential implementations.

\paragraph{Dependence on project-specific context}\noindent

The approach was evaluated on the Berger-Levrault system, whose documentation has a specific organization and whose source code follows established naming and structural conventions. These characteristics provide useful contextual information for locating business-rule implementations, but they may differ in other projects. Applying the approach to other systems may therefore require adapting how documentation and source-code information are combined, depending on the available documentation, naming conventions, and software architecture.
