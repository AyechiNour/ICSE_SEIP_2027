\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

We focus on internal and external validity because the main limitations of our study concern the factors that may affect the evaluation results and the extent to which these results can be generalized beyond the studied system. Internal validity addresses potential influences on the observed results, while external validity considers the applicability of our findings to other systems and contexts.

\subsection{Internal Validity}

\paragraph{Parameter selection.}\noindent

The results may be influenced by the parameter choices used in our approach, particularly the cosine-similarity threshold of 0.7 and the selection of the top-3 business entities for each chunk. Although these values were determined through experimentation, other configurations may lead to different trade-offs between retaining the reference method and reducing the candidate set size.

\paragraph{Ground truth construction.}\noindent

Our evaluation relies on a reference method associated with each business rule, identified using the available documentation and source-code information. To assess the correctness of these associations, we manually verified them by examining the documentation and the corresponding source code. Nevertheless, the reliability of the evaluation depends on the accuracy of the reference methods. If a reference method is incorrectly identified or does not fully represent the implementation of its corresponding rule, the evaluation metrics may not accurately reflect the approach's effectiveness.

\paragraph{Single target method assumption.}\noindent

Our evaluation assumes that each business rule is associated with a single target method. However, a business rule may be implemented across multiple methods that collaborate to realize its logic. Consequently, the approach may identify a method that contributes to the implementation without retaining the specific method designated as the reference. Such a case would be counted as a failure according to our Success Rate, even if part of the rule's implementation had been identified. This assumption may therefore underestimate the effectiveness of the approach for rules whose implementations span multiple methods.

\subsection{External Validity}

\paragraph{Evaluation on a single industrial system.}\noindent

Our evaluation is conducted on a single large industrial Java application developed by Berger-Levrault. Its architecture, business domain, naming conventions, and development practices may differ from those of other legacy systems. Moreover, although our evaluation covers 100 business rules from different parts of the documentation, these rules may not represent the full diversity of business rules encountered in other applications. Further evaluations on systems from different domains are needed to assess the generalizability of our findings.

\paragraph{Dependence on structured documentation.}\noindent

The approach uses contextual information from business-rule documentation to refine the candidate methods. This refinement assumes that a minimum amount of contextual information is available, namely, a file title when business-rule descriptions are stored in a file. Although the approach does not require a highly structured documentation format, the absence of informative file titles may limit the effectiveness of this refinement. Documentation practices vary across projects, and some systems may provide unstructured descriptions or lack meaningful titles that identify the relevant business concepts or functional areas. In such cases, the benefits of documentation-based refinement may be limited, or this step may require adaptation. The source-code-based part of the approach does not depend on this specific documentation structure and can be applied independently.

\paragraph{Dependence on project-specific conventions.}\noindent

The effectiveness of the approach may also depend on conventions specific to the evaluated system, including the representation of business entities in the source code and the correspondence between documentation terminology and source-code names. Other systems may use different naming conventions, architectural patterns, or persistence mechanisms. Applying the approach to such systems may require adapting the identification of business entities and the use of contextual information. Evaluations on additional systems would help determine which parts of the approach can be reused directly and which require adaptation.
