\section{Introduction}

Business rules are operational rules that organizations follow to carry out their activities~\cite{Huan96a}. They define the core logic of business processes and ensure that these processes remain consistent with organizational policies, objectives, and regulations. When implemented in software systems, business rules play a critical role in supporting daily operations~\cite{Cose13a}. They can range from simple validation rules to complex decision-making mechanisms.

However, business rules are rarely implemented as a single, clearly identifiable component of an information system. Instead, they are often scattered across different parts of the source code~\cite{Cose12a}. In long-lived software systems, these rules are also affected by the evolution of the codebase. Systems are developed and maintained over many years by different developers and teams, making their implementations increasingly difficult to understand~\cite{Ayac26a}. Documentation may become incomplete or outdated, while the developers who originally implemented the rules may no longer be available~\cite{Cose13a}. As a result, understanding how a particular business rule is implemented becomes a difficult task.

At the same time, the rules governing organizations continuously evolve. Changes in these rules may result from new market conditions, organizational needs, or regulatory requirements~\cite{Chit19a}. Such changes often require corresponding modifications to the software. Before making a change, developers must identify the parts of the source code that implement the affected business rule. In a large and complex system, this task can be difficult and time-consuming. Developers may spend considerable effort searching for the relevant code before they can even begin implementing the change. This process is also error-prone, as modifying the wrong parts of the code can lead to inconsistent behavior or unintended changes to the system~\cite{Ayac26a}. This situation raises the need for a solution that can assist developers in this task. 

Several studies have investigated traceability between source code and documentation~\cite{Anto02b,Cao18a,Ali11a,Eaddy08a,Keim24a}, aiming to establish links between textual information and the corresponding code elements. However, locating the implementation of a specific business rule from its textual description remains challenging. The challenge is to reduce the search space while preserving the code that implements the rule.

In this paper, we investigate this problem through a real-world case provided by Berger-Levrault, a company that develops information systems for public administration, healthcare, and education. We propose an approach that exploits textual information from business rule descriptions, source code analysis, and documentation analysis to select software methods that are likely to implement a given business rule. By narrowing down the set of candidate methods, the approach helps developers focus their investigation on a smaller portion of the system while preserving a high proportion of the methods that implement the business rule.

An additional challenge concerns the evaluation of such an approach~\cite{Ayac26a}. In real-world software systems, it is uncommon to find projects where business rules are systematically documented and explicitly linked to their implementations in the source code. Without such traceability, establishing a reliable ground truth requires manual investigation, making the evaluation of business rule localization approaches difficult. To enable a reliable evaluation, we therefore focused on a single project provided by our industrial partner. Although the available documentation is limited, it contains sufficient information to reconstruct some links between business rules and their implementations and to establish a ground truth. We assess the proposed approach on this industrial codebase using this ground truth, considering both its ability to retain the methods that implement a business rule and its ability to reduce the number of candidate methods that developers need to inspect.
